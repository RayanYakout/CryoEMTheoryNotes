§1 · Smith ch. 3 · Sampling

Sampling, Nyquist, and why your pixel size is a hard ceiling

A real signal — a sound wave, an electron wave, the projected potential of a protein — is continuous. It has a value at every point. A computer cannot store that, so we sample: measure the value at regular intervals and throw everything in between away.

The obvious question is what gets lost. The answer is surprisingly sharp. If the signal contains no frequency higher than f, and you sample at a rate greater than 2f, then nothing is lost at all — the continuous signal can be reconstructed exactly from the samples. This is the sampling theorem, and the frequency fs/2 (half the sampling rate) is the Nyquist frequency.

The flip side is brutal. Frequencies above Nyquist don't get attenuated or blurred. They get aliased — they fold back into the sampled data disguised as some lower frequency, and once that happens there is no algorithm on earth that can undo it. The high-frequency information isn't degraded; it's actively lying to you.

Interactive 1.1 — watch a sine wave lie to you

The grey curve is the true continuous signal. The dots are your samples. The coloured curve is what a reconstruction algorithm will honestly conclude you measured. Raise the signal frequency past half the sampling rate and watch the two curves part company.

Notice what happens at exactly Nyquist and just past it. At f = fs/2 you're getting two samples per cycle, which is the bare minimum and already fragile. Above it, the apparent frequency starts running backwards: a 12 Hz signal sampled at 20 Hz is indistinguishable from an 8 Hz signal. This is the same effect that makes wagon wheels spin backwards in films.

Cryo-EM payoff · pixel size is a sampling rate

Your detector samples the image plane in space rather than time. The sampling interval is the pixel size, in Å/pixel. Everything above transfers directly:

Nyquist resolution = 2 × pixel size. At 1.06 Å/pixel your absolute hard ceiling is 2.12 Å, no matter how good your particle, how many images you collect, or how expensive the microscope is. At 1.5 Å/pixel it's 3.0 Å. This is why picking a pixel size at the microscope is a decision you cannot walk back later.

Three consequences you meet in practice:

  • Fourier cropping / binning. When cryoSPARC's Extract job offers "Fourier crop to box size", it is literally cropping the FFT — throwing away the outer, high-frequency part of the transform. That doubles or quadruples your pixel size, drops your Nyquist limit, and makes early 2D classification several times faster. Perfectly sane for classification; fatal if you forget to re-extract unbinned before final refinement.
  • Super-resolution mode. K2/K3 detectors can localise electron hits more finely than the physical pixel, effectively halving the pixel size and doubling the Nyquist limit. Reconstructions beyond the physical Nyquist have been demonstrated — but it's demanding, not routine.
  • Your binning error. The class of bug where a box size or pixel size gets applied at the wrong stage is precisely a sampling error: the data is fine, but the software's belief about the sampling interval no longer matches the data's actual sampling interval, so every downstream frequency is misinterpreted.
Sampling relations you should be able to write from memory Nyquist frequency kNyq = 1 / (2 · p)
Nyquist resolution dNyq = 2 · p
Resolution ↔ spatial frequency: d = 1 / k p = pixel size in Å/pixel · k = spatial frequency in Å⁻¹ · d = resolution in Å. Spatial frequency and resolution are reciprocals of each other, which is the single most common source of confusion when reading FSC curves and CTF plots. High frequency = small d = fine detail.
Problem 1.1 — Nyquist from pixel size

You collect on a Krios at a calibrated pixel size of 0.8 Å/pixel. What is the highest resolution you could possibly reach, and what spatial frequency does that correspond to?

Å
Worked solution

Nyquist resolution is twice the sampling interval:

d_Nyq = 2 × p = 2 × 0.8 Å = 1.6 Å

And the corresponding spatial frequency is the reciprocal:

k_Nyq = 1 / 1.6 Å = 0.625 Å⁻¹

→ 1.6 Å, i.e. 0.625 Å⁻¹

In practice you will never approach Nyquist — B-factor decay, beam-induced motion and conformational heterogeneity stop you long before. A common rule of thumb is that you should sample at roughly 1/3 of your target resolution rather than 1/2, giving yourself headroom. At 0.8 Å/pixel, targeting 2.4–3 Å is realistic.

Problem 1.2 — the consequence of binning

You extract particles at 1.06 Å/pixel in a 400-pixel box, then Fourier-crop to a 200-pixel box to speed up 2D classification. What is your new pixel size, and your new Nyquist resolution?

Å
Worked solution

Fourier cropping by a factor of 2 in box size keeps the same physical field of view, so the same real-world distance is now covered by half as many pixels. The pixel size doubles:

p_new = 1.06 × (400 / 200) = 2.12 Å/pixel
d_Nyq = 2 × 2.12 = 4.24 Å

→ 2.12 Å/pixel, Nyquist 4.24 Å

Which is completely fine for 2D classification, where you're sorting on overall shape and views at 10–20 Å. It would be catastrophic if you then ran a final refinement on those cropped particles and reported the resolution — the FSC would flatline at 4.24 Å and you'd have no idea why.

Check yourself

A colleague says: "Our particle only diffracts to 4 Å anyway, so we can afford to bin to 2.5 Å/pixel and save a lot of time." What's the problem with this reasoning?

Nyquist resolution is 2 × 2.5 = 5 Å. Since 5 Å is a coarser resolution than 4 Å, the binned data physically cannot contain the 4 Å information — it's been aliased or discarded. The direction of the inequality catches people out constantly: bigger number in Å means worse resolution. A safe binning would keep Nyquist comfortably finer than the target, e.g. 1.3 Å/pixel giving 2.6 Å Nyquist.
§2 · Smith ch. 2 · Statistics & noise

Why averaging works, and why you need a million particles

Take a measurement with random error. Take it again. The signal is the same both times; the noise is different, and — crucially — uncorrelated. When you add the two measurements, the signal adds coherently (it doubles) while the noise adds incoherently (it grows, but only by √2, because random errors partly cancel).

Run that argument for N measurements and you get the single most important result in all of experimental science:

The √N law SNRaverage of N = √N × SNRsingle Averaging N independent noisy observations of the same underlying signal improves the signal-to-noise ratio by a factor of √N. Note the ratio and the diminishing returns: to double your SNR you need 4× the data; to get 10× you need 100×.

A word on what SNR actually means, because the definition varies by field. In cryo-EM the convention is a variance ratio: SNR = σ²signal / σ²noise. This matters when you compare numbers across papers — some fields use amplitude ratios, which differ by a square.

Cryo-EM payoff · this is the entire justification for single-particle analysis

A raw cryo-EM micrograph has an SNR of roughly 0.1 — estimates in the literature range from about 0.01 to 0.11 depending on definition and conditions. That is far below the threshold for a human to see anything at all. The Rose criterion holds that a feature must sit about five standard deviations above background to be reliably detectable by eye, i.e. SNR ≈ 5.

So to go from SNR 0.1 to SNR 5 you need a factor of 50, and by the √N law that means N ≈ 2,500 particles. That's the number to make a class average visible. Getting an isotropic high-resolution 3D reconstruction needs far more — not only for SNR, but because every particle contributes only one orientation, and you need to fill three-dimensional Fourier space densely from every direction. Hence the 1.88 million particles from your 18,006 micrographs: that's not excess, it's the requirement.

The same principle operates one level down. Each micrograph is itself a dose-fractionated movie, and motion correction aligns and averages those frames. Same √N, applied to frames instead of particles.

Problem 2.1 — how many particles to see anything

Your single-particle images have SNR ≈ 0.08. How many particles must you average to reach the Rose criterion of SNR = 5?

particles
Worked solution

You need a gain factor G:

G = 5 / 0.08 = 62.5

The gain is √N, so:

√N = 62.5 → N = 62.5² = 3906

→ about 3,900 particles

Which is roughly the size of a decent 2D class — and that is not a coincidence. It's why 2D class averages look like recognisable projections while individual particle picks look like noise.

Problem 2.2 — the cost of the last increment

You have 400,000 particles. Your collaborator suggests collecting more to improve the reconstruction. Ignoring all other factors (heterogeneity, B-factor, orientation coverage), how many particles would you need in total to improve the SNR by a factor of 1.5?

particles
Worked solution

SNR scales as √N, so to multiply SNR by 1.5 you multiply N by 1.5²:

N_new = 400,000 × 1.5² = 400,000 × 2.25 = 900,000

→ 900,000 particles, i.e. another 500,000 on top of what you have

This is the honest arithmetic behind "should we collect more data?" More than doubling the dataset buys you a 50% SNR improvement. That is often not the best use of microscope time compared with, say, fixing a preferred-orientation problem or improving sample quality — both of which can buy far more than 1.5×. The √N law is why data collection alone has such punishing diminishing returns.

Check yourself

Averaging improves SNR by √N only under one critical assumption. Which?

Independence of noise plus identity of signal. Gaussianity is not required (though it's usually a good approximation). This assumption is exactly what fails in the two hardest problems in SPA: misalignment (the signal isn't in the same place in each image, so it too adds incoherently and blurs) and heterogeneity (the signal genuinely isn't identical, because the particles are in different conformational states). Both cause averaging to destroy the very detail you're trying to recover — and both are why 3D classification exists.
§3 · Smith ch. 5 · Linear systems

Linearity: the property that makes all of this legal

A system is anything that takes a signal in and gives a signal out. A microscope is a system. So is a filter, a detector, a reconstruction algorithm. A system is linear if it satisfies two conditions:

  • Homogeneity — scale the input by k, and the output scales by k. Twice the specimen density gives twice the image contrast.
  • Additivity (superposition) — feed in the sum of two inputs and you get the sum of the two outputs. Two molecules side by side produce the sum of what each would produce alone.

A third property, shift invariance, says that moving the input moves the output by the same amount without otherwise changing it. A system that is both linear and shift-invariant is called LSI, and LSI systems are the ones we know how to reason about completely.

Why do we care so much? Because linearity licenses decomposition. If a system is linear, you can chop an impossibly complicated input into simple pieces, push each piece through separately, and add up the results. Two decompositions dominate everything that follows:

Cryo-EM payoff · the microscope is an LSI system

The whole edifice of cryo-EM image processing rests on modelling the transmission electron microscope as approximately linear and shift-invariant. That approximation is what allows us to write image formation as a single convolution, to correct the CTF by a per-pixel operation in Fourier space, and to combine thousands of independently-recorded images into one reconstruction.

It's worth knowing where the approximation strains. Shift invariance fails across a large field of view when the defocus varies with position — which is exactly why cryoSPARC has patch CTF estimation and local motion correction rather than one global value. The fix is to carve the micrograph into patches small enough that shift invariance holds within each one.

§4 · Smith ch. 6–7 · Convolution

Convolution, the impulse response, and the point spread function

Start with the simplest possible input: a single spike of unit height at one position, zero everywhere else. This is the delta function, δ. Whatever an LSI system does to a delta function is called its impulse response, written h. Because of linearity plus shift invariance, the impulse response tells you everything about the system — if you know how it smears one point, you know how it treats any input at all.

The reason is the input-side view of convolution. Any signal is just a row of scaled, shifted deltas. Push each one through: each becomes a scaled, shifted copy of h. Add them all up. That summation is convolution, written with an asterisk:

Convolution y[n] = x[n] ∗ h[n] = Σk x[k] · h[n − k] x = input · h = impulse response · y = output. The formula says: for each output point n, slide the impulse response so its origin sits at n, multiply it against the input point by point, and sum. The two ways of reading it — "scatter each input" (input-side) and "gather for each output" (output-side) — give identical results and are worth being able to switch between.

Try the oscillating kernel. Notice that convolution with something that rings doesn't just blur — it produces dark fringes beside bright features, and features can even invert. In two dimensions the same operation applies with a 2D kernel. The 2D impulse response of an imaging system has its own name: the point spread function (PSF). It's the image you'd get from an infinitely small point of specimen — the system's signature blur.

Cryo-EM payoff · the image formation model

The core equation of cryo-EM image formation is a convolution:

image = (projected potential) ∗ PSF + noise

Your micrograph is not a picture of the protein. It is the protein's projected Coulomb potential, convolved with the microscope's point spread function, buried in noise at SNR 0.1. Every step of processing after that is an attempt to undo one of those three operations: CTF correction undoes the convolution, averaging beats the noise, and 3D reconstruction undoes the projection.

The Fourier transform of the PSF is the contrast transfer function. That's §9 — but the reason the CTF is such a central object is exactly this: it is the frequency-domain face of the convolution that defines your data.

Check yourself

Why does knowing a system's impulse response tell you its response to any input?

Exactly this. Decomposition into impulses, plus linearity and shift invariance, is the entire argument. And the last option is a trap worth noticing: the frequency response isn't independent information at all — it is the Fourier transform of the impulse response. Same object, different coordinates. PSF and CTF are a Fourier pair.
§5 · Smith ch. 8–9 · The DFT

The Discrete Fourier Transform: any signal is a pile of sine waves

The second decomposition strategy. Instead of breaking a signal into impulses, break it into sinusoids — sines and cosines of increasing frequency. The claim, which feels implausible the first time you meet it, is that any signal can be written as a weighted sum of sinusoids, including signals with sharp corners and flat regions that look nothing like waves.

The Discrete Fourier Transform is the version of this a computer can compute: it takes N samples in and produces N/2+1 amplitude values and N/2+1 phase values, describing how much of each frequency the signal contains and where each of those waves sits.

The spike preset is worth dwelling on. To build a single narrow spike you need every frequency, all at equal amplitude. That is the reciprocal relationship in its purest form: narrow in real space means broad in Fourier space, and vice versa. A delta function transforms to a flat spectrum. A flat constant transforms to a delta. This is a rule you will use constantly.

The four Fourier transforms, and which one you actually use

Smith makes a point that saves a lot of confusion later. There are four Fourier transforms, not one, depending on whether your signal is continuous or discrete and whether it's periodic or aperiodic:

Signal typeTransformWhere you meet it
Continuous, aperiodicFourier TransformTheory and derivations — the CTF equation is written this way
Continuous, periodicFourier SeriesCrystallography, helical symmetry, layer lines
Discrete, aperiodicDTFTFilter design theory; rarely computed directly
Discrete, periodicDFTEverything you actually run. Every FFT of a micrograph

The DFT is the only one a computer can evaluate, and it assumes your data is periodic — that the signal wraps around and repeats forever. Your 400×400 particle box is treated as one tile of an infinite wallpaper. That assumption is invisible most of the time and occasionally bites hard.

Where the periodicity assumption bites · spectral leakage

If your signal doesn't fit a whole number of cycles into the window, the DFT sees a discontinuity where the end wraps around to the beginning. A sharp edge, as we just established, contains every frequency — so energy smears across the whole spectrum. This is spectral leakage.

The fix in DSP is windowing: taper the signal smoothly to zero at the edges before transforming. In cryo-EM the same fix has different names — the soft circular mask applied to particle boxes, and the apodisation of micrographs before computing power spectra. A hard-edged square box produces a cross-shaped artefact through the origin of the power spectrum, which is pure leakage from the box edges. It's why every extraction job applies a soft-edged mask by default, and why sharpening a mask edge too aggressively creates spurious high-resolution correlation.

Cryo-EM payoff · you already look at DFTs every day

Every power spectrum you inspect during a data collection is a DFT — specifically the squared amplitude of the 2D DFT of a micrograph. The Thon rings you judge quality by are the visible oscillations of the CTF, read directly off that spectrum.

When you assess a micrograph by eye, you are reading a frequency-domain plot: ring spacing tells you defocus, ring ellipticity tells you astigmatism, how far out the rings extend tells you the effective resolution of that image, and a smeared or asymmetric pattern tells you about drift or ice thickness. Learning to read power spectra fluently is learning to read a DFT.

Problem 5.1 — Fourier pixel spacing

You extract particles into a 256-pixel box at 1.06 Å/pixel. What is the spacing between adjacent pixels in the Fourier transform, in Å⁻¹?

Å⁻¹
Worked solution

The Fourier transform of an N-pixel box samples frequency space at intervals of 1 divided by the total physical extent of the box:

box extent = N × p = 256 × 1.06 Å = 271.4 Å
Δk = 1 / (N · p) = 1 / 271.4 Å = 0.003684 Å⁻¹

→ 0.00368 Å⁻¹ per Fourier pixel

This is why box size matters beyond just "fitting the particle in". A larger box gives finer sampling of Fourier space — reciprocal again. That finer sampling is what allows accurate CTF fitting near the origin and clean separation of delocalised signal at high defocus. It's also why the standard advice is to make the box roughly 1.5–2× the particle diameter rather than snugly fitting it: the extra empty space buys you Fourier resolution.

Problem 5.2 — choosing a box size

Your particle is about 140 Å across and your pixel size is 0.8 Å/pixel. Using the 1.5–2× rule and rounding to a good FFT size, what box size (in pixels) would you extract at?

px
Worked solution

Take about 1.8× the particle diameter for the physical box:

box extent ≈ 1.8 × 140 Å = 252 Å
box in pixels = 252 / 0.8 = 315 px

Now round to a size that FFTs efficiently — an even number whose prime factors are all small (2, 3, 5, 7). 315 is odd; 320 = 2⁶ × 5 is an excellent choice, and 336 = 2⁴ × 3 × 7 also works.

→ 320 px (256 would be too tight at 205 Å; 384 is safe but slower)

Anything within roughly 260–400 px is defensible here — this is a judgement call, not a formula. At high defocus you want the larger end, because CTF delocalisation spreads signal further from the particle centre than you'd expect. Avoid prime or near-prime box sizes: an FFT of size 317 is dramatically slower than one of size 320, for reasons that become clear in §8.

§6 · Amplitude and phase

Phases carry the picture

A DFT gives you two numbers per frequency. You can store them as real and imaginary parts, or — usually more intuitive — as amplitude and phase. Amplitude says how much of that frequency is present. Phase says where the wave sits: whether its peak lands here or a quarter-wavelength over there.

People's instinct is that amplitude is the important half. It isn't. There's a classic demonstration, due to Kevin Cowtan, that settles the question immediately: take two images, Fourier transform both, then swap their amplitudes while keeping each one's phases, and transform back.

The hybrids follow their phases, not their amplitudes. Amplitude contributes texture and contrast; phase contributes structure, position, and identity. If you had to throw one away, you would keep the phases without hesitating.

Cryo-EM payoff · why phase errors are the enemy

Several things follow directly:

  • Phase flipping is a legitimate CTF correction. The simplest CTF correction just fixes the sign of the transform where the CTF went negative, leaving amplitudes untouched. That sounds crude — it fixes only half the problem — but since phases carry the structure, it recovers most of what matters. It's still used when you want to preserve noise statistics.
  • Misalignment is a phase error. By the shift theorem (§7), a translational error in a particle's assigned position is exactly a phase ramp applied to its transform. Averaging misaligned particles means averaging inconsistent phases, which cancels signal. This is the mathematical statement of why alignment accuracy sets your resolution limit.
  • The FSC is a phase-sensitive measurement. It compares complex Fourier coefficients between half-maps, so two maps with matching power spectra but random relative phases score zero. FSC measures whether your two halves agree about structure, not just about how much power sits at each frequency.
  • Sharpening cannot invent structure. B-factor sharpening rescales amplitudes only. It never touches phases. Which is precisely why it can make an existing map look crisper but can never add information that isn't there — a point worth being able to make confidently when someone over-sharpens.
Check yourself

A map is sharpened with a B-factor of −100 Å². Which statement is correct?

Sharpening is a pure amplitude-scaling operation in Fourier space, designed to counteract the envelope decay of the microscope and the specimen. It restores the relative weighting of frequencies so that features you already resolved become visible. It adds nothing. Over-sharpening amplifies high-frequency noise along with signal — which is why maps can look simultaneously crisper and less interpretable, and why local sharpening approaches (LocScale, LocalDeblur) exist to avoid applying one global factor to a map whose resolution varies.
§7 · Smith ch. 10–11 · Properties

Four properties that do most of the work

The Fourier transform has a long list of properties. Four of them account for nearly everything you do in cryo-EM, and they're worth memorising outright.

1. The convolution theorem

The most useful identity in imaging x(r) ∗ h(r) ⟷ X(k) · H(k) Convolution in real space is multiplication in Fourier space. And the reverse: multiplication in real space is convolution in Fourier space. This single fact turns an expensive sliding-and-summing operation into a cheap element-by-element product.

This is why the CTF matters more than the PSF in practice. The image formation model in real space is an awkward convolution; transform it, and it becomes a simple multiplication of the specimen's transform by the CTF. Correcting it becomes a division (or a sign flip, or a Wiener-weighted division) rather than a deconvolution.

2. The shift theorem

Movement is phase x(r − r₀) ⟷ X(k) · e−2πi k·r₀ Shifting a signal in real space multiplies its transform by a linear phase ramp. Amplitudes are completely unchanged — only phases move. Reading it in reverse: applying a phase ramp in Fourier space shifts an image in real space, by any amount you like, including fractions of a pixel.

This is how MotionCor2 and every particle-alignment routine apply fractional shifts without blurring the data.

3. Reciprocity and scaling

Stretch a signal in real space and its transform compresses, and vice versa. Narrow features need broad spectra. Already met in §5 with the spike, but worth naming: it explains why small particles are hard (their transforms are broad and low-amplitude), why a tight mask in real space causes broad correlations in Fourier space, and why a low-pass filter — cutting off the outer part of Fourier space — necessarily smooths real space.

4. Hermitian (Friedel) symmetry

If your input is real-valued — and a micrograph is just real numbers, there's no imaginary intensity — then its transform has a symmetry: F(−k) = F*(k). The value at any point equals the complex conjugate of the value diametrically opposite through the origin. Amplitudes are mirror-symmetric; phases are antisymmetric.

Practically this means half the transform is redundant. Software stores and computes only half, which halves memory and roughly halves the work. In crystallography the same relation is called Friedel's law. When you see a power spectrum with perfect two-fold symmetry, that's not a property of your specimen — it's a mathematical guarantee.

Check yourself

You want to test 500 different trial shifts of one image against a reference, to find the best alignment. Why does no one do this by direct comparison?

This is the single biggest computational win in the whole pipeline. Cross-correlation via FFT computes the score for every possible shift simultaneously: forward-transform both, multiply one by the conjugate of the other, inverse-transform, and the resulting map's peak position is the best shift. Testing 500 shifts costs the same as testing 5. §12 goes through this properly.
§8 · Smith ch. 12 · The FFT

Why the FFT changed everything

The DFT as written in §5 requires, for each of N output frequencies, a sum over all N input points. That's N² operations. The Fast Fourier Transform is an algorithm — not a different transform, the exact same answer — that exploits redundancy in those sums to get the job done in N log₂N operations.

For small N the difference is unimpressive. For the sizes we actually use, it's the difference between possible and impossible.

Cryo-EM payoff · why box sizes look the way they do

The classic FFT algorithm works by repeatedly splitting the transform in half, which is why powers of two are ideal. Modern libraries (FFTW, cuFFT) handle other sizes by factorising them — but only efficiently if the factors are small primes: 2, 3, 5, 7.

That's the whole reason for the peculiar list of "good" cryo-EM box sizes. 256 = 2⁸ and 320 = 2⁶ × 5 are excellent. 336 = 2⁴ × 3 × 7 is fine. But 314 = 2 × 157 contains a large prime factor and will run dramatically slower, because the library has to fall back to a general algorithm for that factor. If a job is mysteriously slow and the box size is odd or prime, that's the first thing to check.

The same logic explains a rule you may have absorbed without justification: always use even box sizes. Odd sizes complicate the placement of the Fourier origin and are poorly optimised almost everywhere.

§9 · The contrast transfer function

The CTF: what your microscope actually does to information

Everything so far converges here. The microscope is an LSI system (§3). Its impulse response is the PSF, and image formation is a convolution (§4). By the convolution theorem (§7), that convolution becomes a multiplication in Fourier space, and the thing you multiply by is the Fourier transform of the PSF: the contrast transfer function.

The CTF tells you, for each spatial frequency, how much of the specimen's information reaches your image and with what sign. It is not a blur factor. It is an oscillating function that transfers some frequencies strongly, some weakly, some with inverted contrast, and some — at its zero crossings — not at all.

The CTF (CTFFIND4 / RELION convention) CTF(k) = −[ √(1 − A²) · sin χ(k) + A · cos χ(k) ]

χ(k) = π · λ · Δz · k² − (π/2) · Cs · λ³ · k⁴ + Δφ k spatial frequency (Å⁻¹) · λ electron wavelength (Å) · Δz defocus (Å, underfocus positive) · Cs spherical aberration (Å; 2.7 mm = 2.7×10⁷ Å) · A fractional amplitude contrast (≈0.07–0.10 in vitreous ice) · Δφ extra phase shift (0 normally, ≈π/2 with a Volta phase plate).

χ is the wave aberration function — the phase error the microscope introduces at frequency k. The k² term is defocus, which you control. The k⁴ term is spherical aberration, fixed by the lens. Note the sign convention: packages differ, so always check before comparing numbers across software.

Reading the plot

Five things to notice, each of which is a decision you make at the microscope:

  • The zeros are real information loss. Where CTF(k) = 0, that frequency is multiplied by zero. It isn't attenuated — it's gone. No filter can recover it from that image, because zero times anything is zero.
  • Higher defocus pushes the first zero to lower resolution while boosting low-frequency contrast. That's the fundamental trade: high defocus makes particles easy to see and pick, but truncates useful information earlier. Low defocus preserves high-resolution transfer but gives images where the particles are nearly invisible.
  • Collecting over a defocus range fixes the zeros. The zeros sit at different frequencies for different defoci. Combine images across a range and every frequency is well-transferred by some subset of your data. This is why you set a defocus range rather than a single value, and why a dataset all collected at one defocus has permanent holes in it.
  • The envelope kills you before the zeros do. Turn up the B-factor and watch the oscillations damp out. This envelope — from partial coherence, energy spread, beam-induced motion and detector DQE — is what actually sets your practical resolution limit, and it's what sharpening later tries to compensate.
  • Astigmatism makes the CTF two-dimensional. Defocus becomes direction-dependent, circular rings become ellipses, and a single 1D CTF no longer describes the image. This is why CTF estimation fits an astigmatic model, and why badly astigmatic images can't be salvaged by a scalar defocus correction.

Three approaches, in increasing sophistication:

Problem 9.1 — electron wavelength

Compute the relativistic electron wavelength at 300 kV. Use λ = h / √(2m₀eV(1 + eV/2m₀c²)), with the relativistic correction term eV/2m₀c² = 300/1022 = 0.2935.

Å
Worked solution

The non-relativistic shortcut first, as a sanity check:

λ ≈ 12.26 / √V = 12.26 / √300000 = 0.0224 Å

That's an overestimate, because at 300 kV the electron is moving fast enough that relativity matters — its mass is effectively 1.29× the rest mass. Applying the correction:

λ = 12.26 / √(V · (1 + 0.978×10⁻⁶ V)) = 12.26 / √(300000 × 1.2935)
λ = 12.26 / √388050 = 12.26 / 622.9 = 0.0197 Å

→ λ = 0.0197 Å at 300 kV

Worth committing to memory: 0.0197 Å at 300 kV, 0.0251 Å at 200 kV, 0.0370 Å at 100 kV. Note that the wavelength is roughly a hundred times smaller than the resolution you achieve — the limit is emphatically not diffraction, it's everything else.

Problem 9.2 — the first CTF zero

At 300 kV (λ = 0.0197 Å) with 1.0 µm underfocus, at what resolution does the first CTF zero fall? Ignore Cs and amplitude contrast — at the first zero both are negligible.

Å
Worked solution

Zeros of the CTF occur where χ(k) = nπ. Dropping the Cs term, the first zero (n = 1) is at:

π · λ · Δz · k² = π → k₁ = √( 1 / (λ · Δz) )

Watch the units — convert defocus to Ångström:

Δz = 1.0 µm = 10⁴ Å
λ · Δz = 0.0197 × 10⁴ = 197 Å²
k₁ = √(1/197) = 0.0712 Å⁻¹

And the resolution is the reciprocal, which conveniently simplifies:

d₁ = 1/k₁ = √(λ · Δz) = √197 = 14.0 Å

→ first zero at 14.0 Å

The compact form d₁ = √(λ · Δz) is worth remembering — it lets you estimate the first zero in your head at the microscope. And note the √ scaling: at 2 µm defocus the first zero moves to √394 = 19.8 Å, a factor of √2 worse. Doubling defocus does not halve your first zero resolution.

Check yourself

Someone proposes collecting an entire dataset at a single defocus of 1.2 µm, for consistency. What's wrong?

The zeros would coincide across every single micrograph, leaving permanent gaps in the transferred spatial frequencies. Averaging a million particles doesn't help, because zero times anything is still zero. A defocus range — typically something like 0.8–2.0 µm — staggers the zeros so that every frequency is well-transferred somewhere in the dataset, and the reconstruction fills the gaps by combining across defoci.
§10 · Projection-slice

The theorem that makes 3D reconstruction possible

Here is the problem. Your specimen is three-dimensional. Your images are two-dimensional projections — every image is the whole 3D object flattened along the beam direction. How do you get the 3D object back?

The answer is a single theorem, and it is the mathematical foundation of both single-particle analysis and tomography:

The projection-slice theorem (central section theorem) FT2D[ projection of f along direction n̂ ] = central slice through FT3D[ f ] ⊥ n̂ The 2D Fourier transform of a projection image equals a flat plane cut through the 3D Fourier transform of the object — a plane passing through the origin, perpendicular to the direction you projected along.

Read that again slowly, because it's doing enormous work. It says a 2D image gives you a 2D plane of the 3D Fourier volume, for free, just by taking its FFT. Collect projections from many directions, transform each one, drop each into the 3D Fourier volume at the right orientation, and once you've filled the volume you inverse-transform it and you have your structure.

Cryo-EM payoff · two techniques, one theorem

In SPA, every particle is the same object seen from a random orientation. Determining a particle's orientation is exactly determining which plane of Fourier space it contributes. Refinement is an iterative search: guess orientations, build a volume, re-compare, update. The reconstruction step itself is just inserting slices at the right angles and inverse-transforming.

In tomography, you have one object and you deliberately tilt it, collecting a known set of orientations. Your 114 tilt series each contribute a fan of central planes at known angles. Same theorem, but the angles are given rather than searched for — which is why tomographic reconstruction is fast and SPA alignment is expensive.

Two more things fall out. Weighted back-projection is a real-space way of doing the same insertion; the "weighting" corrects for the fact that central planes all pass through the origin and therefore oversample low frequencies while undersampling high ones. And common lines: any two central planes intersect along a line through the origin, so two projections of the same object must agree along that line. That constraint is enough to determine relative orientations from scratch, which is the basis of ab initio reconstruction.

Check yourself

Why does preferred orientation limit resolution even when you have millions of particles?

Adding more particles at an orientation you already have improves the SNR of a plane you already measured — it does nothing for the planes you're missing. The result is anisotropic resolution: sharp in the well-sampled directions, smeared in the others. It's the same geometry as the missing wedge in tomography, and the remedies are physical rather than computational: tilted collection, detergent or support-film changes, or altering the air-water interface behaviour.
§11 · The missing wedge

Why tomograms are blurred in Z

In tomography you tilt the specimen and record a projection at each angle. By the projection-slice theorem, each tilt contributes a central plane at that angle. If you could tilt through the full ±90°, you'd fill Fourier space completely.

You can't. As you tilt, the effective thickness of the specimen grows as 1/cos(θ) — at 60° the beam is passing through twice as much ice as at 0°, and at 80° nearly six times as much. Beyond about 60–70° the images become unusable, and the holder physically collides with the pole piece. So the tilt range stops at roughly ±60°, and the unmeasured angles leave a wedge-shaped void in Fourier space.

Every artefact you've noticed in a tomogram traces back to this wedge:

  • Elongation in Z. Spherical objects become ellipsoids stretched along the beam axis. Membranes perpendicular to the beam look crisp; membranes parallel to it fade or vanish entirely. That's not sample damage — it's missing data.
  • Anisotropic resolution. Your resolution is genuinely direction-dependent, best in-plane and worst along Z. A single resolution number for a tomogram is misleading.
  • Why subtomogram averaging works so well. If your particles sit in the tomogram at many different orientations, each copy has its missing wedge pointing in a different direction relative to the particle. Averaging them fills in each other's wedges. This is the central reason subtomogram averaging can exceed what any single tomogram shows — and why particles that all sit in the same orientation (say, lying flat on a membrane) average much less well.
  • Dose-symmetric tilt schemes. The Hagen scheme collects low tilts first, when the specimen is least damaged, because those tilts carry the most information per image. It's an explicit acknowledgement that not all tilts are equal.

Deep-learning methods (IsoNet and relatives) attempt to fill the wedge by learning what plausible density looks like. Useful for visualisation and segmentation; treat inferred density with appropriate suspicion when making structural claims.

Problem 11.1 — how much of Fourier space do you actually have?

You collect a tilt series over ±60°. What fraction of the full angular range in the tilt plane have you sampled?

%
Worked solution

The full range needed is ±90°, i.e. 180°. You covered ±60°, i.e. 120°:

fraction = 120° / 180° = 0.667 = 66.7%

→ two-thirds sampled, one-third missing

A third of Fourier space simply absent — which is a much larger deficit than most people picture when they hear "missing wedge". Pushing to ±70° gets you to 78%, which is why high-tilt schemes are worth the effort when the sample is thin enough to survive them. Going the other way, a ±40° range leaves you with only 44%, and the reconstruction becomes hard to interpret in Z at all.

§12 · Cross-correlation

How everything gets aligned

Alignment is the operation you run more than any other. Motion correction aligns movie frames. Particle picking matches templates. 2D classification aligns particles to class averages. Refinement aligns particles to projections of a 3D model. Tilt-series alignment registers tilts to each other. All of it is cross-correlation, and all of it runs on the convolution theorem.

Naively: to find how much image B is shifted relative to image A, try every possible shift, compute a similarity score for each, and take the best. For a 400×400 image that's 160,000 trial shifts, each requiring 160,000 multiplications. Utterly impractical.

Via the convolution theorem, the entire cross-correlation map — the score at every possible shift, simultaneously — comes from:

Cross-correlation by FFT CC(r) = FT⁻¹[ A(k) · B*(k) ] Transform both images, multiply one by the complex conjugate of the other, inverse-transform. The position of the peak in the result is the shift; its height is the quality of the match. Cost: three FFTs and one multiply, regardless of how many shifts you're searching over.

Cryo-EM payoff · where you meet this
  • Motion correction. MotionCor2 cross-correlates movie frames to measure beam-induced motion, then applies the measured shifts. Because it works in patches, it corrects anisotropic motion across the field. The sub-pixel shifts are applied via the shift theorem — a phase ramp, not interpolation.
  • Template-based particle picking. Classical pickers cross-correlate templates against the micrograph and threshold the peaks. crYOLO takes a different route — a trained convolutional network rather than explicit correlation — which is why it handles crowded and low-contrast micrographs better, and why its failure modes are different (it fails on things unlike its training data rather than on things that correlate poorly).
  • Refinement. Every iteration compares each particle against many projections of the current model. The translational part of that search is cross-correlation; the rotational part is a discrete search over angles. This is why refinement cost scales with angular sampling but barely with box size — the shifts come for free.
  • Sub-pixel accuracy. The correlation peak can be located to a fraction of a pixel by fitting its shape or by upsampling the transform around the peak. This is not a refinement of a discrete answer; it's genuinely continuous information available in the phases.
§13 · FSC

Resolution: what the number actually means

"3.2 Å" is a strange kind of number. It isn't measured with a ruler and it isn't a property of your map alone — it's a statement about reproducibility. The measurement that produces it is the Fourier Shell Correlation.

The procedure: split your particles randomly into two halves and refine each half completely independently. Reconstruct two maps. Fourier transform both. Then, for each spherical shell of spatial frequency, compute the correlation between the two maps' complex Fourier coefficients:

Fourier Shell Correlation FSC(k) = Σshell F₁ · F₂* / √( Σshell|F₁|² · Σshell|F₂|² ) Summed over all voxels in the shell at spatial frequency k. The result runs from 1 (the halves agree perfectly at that frequency) down to 0 (they're uncorrelated — whatever is there is noise). Because the coefficients are complex, this is sensitive to phase agreement, not just to matching power.

Plot FSC against spatial frequency and you get a curve that starts near 1 at low frequency and falls to zero at high frequency. Your reported resolution is where it crosses a threshold — conventionally 0.143.

Why 0.143, and why gold-standard

The 0.143 threshold comes from Rosenthal and Henderson's 2003 analysis, which asked what level of half-map correlation corresponds to a map with enough information to be interpretable — roughly, the point at which the map's information content is comparable to what a crystallographic structure at that resolution would carry. Van Heel and Schatz later noted that 0.143 is numerically close to their independently-derived half-bit information criterion, which is reassuring but not the same argument.

The important thing is that 0.143 is a convention grounded in a statistical argument, not a physical constant. It assumes the two half-maps are genuinely independent. The older 0.5 threshold is more conservative and is still used for map-to-model comparisons.

Where FSC misleads
  • Correlated half-maps. If both halves were refined against a common reference, or if particles from the same micrograph ended up split across halves in a correlated way, the halves aren't independent and the FSC is optimistic. This is exactly why gold-standard refinement — completely independent processing from a low-pass filtered starting point — became the standard.
  • Tight masks. A mask with a sharp edge correlates the noise inside it between the two halves, inflating the FSC at high frequency. Move the mask slider on the interactive above and watch the tail lift artificially. The correction is phase randomisation above a chosen frequency, which measures how much of the FSC the mask alone is responsible for and subtracts it.
  • It's one number for a heterogeneous map. A well-ordered core and a floppy peripheral domain give one global FSC that describes neither. Local resolution estimation applies FSC in moving windows and produces a map of resolution, which is nearly always more informative than the global figure.
  • Imposed symmetry. Applying symmetry averages equivalent regions, raising the FSC. Legitimate if the symmetry is real; badly misleading if it isn't.
Check yourself

Your FSC curve has a long flat tail near 0.2 that suddenly drops at Nyquist. What should you suspect first?

A flat elevated tail is the signature of a masking artefact. Real signal decays; a tail that just sits at some constant value is usually the mask correlating noise between the halves. RELION's post-processing runs phase randomisation automatically to measure and subtract this. If the corrected curve drops away cleanly, the tail was the mask; if it survives, you may have real signal. Either way the tail is a thing to investigate rather than to report.
§14 · Filtering

Low-pass, high-pass, and sharpening

Now that Fourier space is a place you can reason about, filtering is trivial to describe: multiply the transform by something, transform back. What you multiply by determines the filter.

  • Low-pass — keep low frequencies, suppress high. Smooths and denoises. Used constantly: initial models are low-pass filtered to 40–60 Å to prevent refinement from locking onto noise features; maps are filtered to their measured resolution before display so you're not looking at frequencies the data doesn't support.
  • High-pass — remove low frequencies. Flattens background gradients from uneven ice or illumination.
  • Band-pass — keep a range. Used in particle picking to suppress both background gradients and high-frequency noise, leaving the size scale of your particle.

Always taper the filter edge. A hard cutoff in Fourier space is a sharp edge, and a sharp edge in one domain means ringing in the other (§5, reciprocity again). Hard-cut low-pass filters produce visible ripples around features in real space.

Sharpening

Your map's high-frequency amplitudes are systematically too weak. The microscope's envelope, beam-induced motion, and residual misalignment all attenuate high frequencies, so a correctly reconstructed map still looks blurrier than its FSC resolution suggests. Sharpening compensates by boosting those amplitudes:

B-factor sharpening Fsharp(k) = F(k) · exp( −B · k² / 4 ) with B negative for sharpening (e.g. −100 Å²). Applied to amplitudes only — phases are untouched. The value of B is usually estimated from the slope of a Guinier plot (log amplitude against k²), following Rosenthal & Henderson.

Sharpening rescales amplitudes. It does not create information, correct phases, or improve resolution. What it does is restore the relative weighting between frequencies so that detail already present in the map becomes visible to your eye and to model-building software.

Over-sharpening amplifies noise along with signal and produces maps that look crisp but contain spurious features — broken density, false side-chain-like blobs. Because the useful B varies across a map (a rigid core and a flexible periphery need different treatment), local sharpening methods (LocScale, LocalDeblur, Phenix AutoSharpen) apply a resolution-dependent scaling instead of one global factor. For a map with substantial flexibility, local sharpening is usually worth the extra step.

Mixed problem set

These mix the sections. Work them on paper before opening the solutions — the point is the reasoning chain, not the arithmetic.

Problem A — a full calculation chain

You are at a 200 kV Glacios (λ = 0.0251 Å), Cs = 2.7 mm, pixel size 1.2 Å/pixel, defocus 1.5 µm. (i) What is your Nyquist resolution? (ii) Where is the first CTF zero? (iii) Is the first zero inside or outside your Nyquist limit?

Å
Worked solution

(i) Nyquist:

d_Nyq = 2 × 1.2 = 2.4 Å

(ii) First zero, using d₁ = √(λ·Δz) with Δz = 1.5 µm = 15,000 Å:

λ · Δz = 0.0251 × 15000 = 376.5 Å²
d₁ = √376.5 = 19.4 Å

(iii) 19.4 Å is a much coarser resolution than 2.4 Å, so the first zero falls well inside the resolution range you can sample. There will be many CTF oscillations between 19.4 Å and Nyquist — which is exactly what you want, because it means you have plenty of Thon rings to fit and CTF estimation will be well-constrained.

→ Nyquist 2.4 Å; first zero 19.4 Å; many oscillations in between

If the first zero ever fell beyond Nyquist — which happens at very low defocus and coarse pixel size — you'd see no Thon rings at all, and CTF estimation would fail with no clear error message. That's a real failure mode worth recognising.

Problem B — diagnosing a stall

Your refinement plateaus at 8 Å with 300,000 particles. A collaborator suggests collecting 300,000 more. Assuming the limit were purely SNR (it usually isn't), what resolution improvement would doubling the dataset buy, in terms of SNR gain?

×
Worked solution
gain = √(600,000 / 300,000) = √2 = 1.41×

→ a 41% SNR improvement for double the microscope time

And that translates into a much smaller resolution gain, because resolution depends on SNR through the steep exponential of the B-factor envelope, not linearly. More to the point: a plateau at 8 Å with 300k particles is almost never an SNR problem. The usual culprits are conformational heterogeneity (needs 3D classification), preferred orientation (needs a Fourier-space coverage fix, §10), or alignment error (needs better initial models or per-particle CTF refinement). The √N calculation is most useful as an argument for not collecting more data until you've ruled those out.

Problem C — the tomography version

Your tilt series runs ±60° in 3° increments. How many tilts is that, and what fraction of Fourier space in the tilt plane do you cover?

tilts

With 114 tilt series at 41 tilts each you're handling around 4,700 individual images — which is why the tomography pipeline is so much more bookkeeping-heavy than SPA, and why per-tilt-series metadata errors are the dominant failure mode. Note also that the tilt increment controls how finely you sample within the wedge, while the tilt range controls how big the wedge is. They're different problems: a finer increment won't fix a narrow range.

Synthesis

Trace the chain: why is the CTF a multiplication in Fourier space rather than something more complicated?

That chain — linearity → convolution → convolution theorem → multiplication — is the spine of this whole module. If you can reconstruct it unprompted, you have the conceptual core. Everything else in cryo-EM image processing hangs off it: CTF correction is undoing that multiplication, the projection-slice theorem tells you which part of Fourier space each image occupies, and FSC measures how well two independent attempts at the reconstruction agree.

A closing note on the gap

The feeling that algorithm discussions are a foreign language is not a sign that something went wrong. Software like cryoSPARC and RELION is deliberately built so you don't need any of this to get a structure — that's the point of it. Which means operational competence arrives first and theoretical fluency lags behind, for nearly everyone who learns the practice before the maths.

What closes the gap is exposure at a steady rate, not intensity. An hour a day for a few weeks and the vocabulary in this module stops being vocabulary and starts being how you think about your data. The material isn't hard so much as it is unfamiliar, and unfamiliarity has a reliable cure.
